Turn a paper page, and a virtual page turns with it. The pleasure is immediate: something in your hands has crossed into another world without losing its familiar shape.

In my experiments for Histy, I have prototyped this kind of synchronized page turning. It gives me a concrete interaction to think with. It does not, by itself, tell me whether anyone understands a book better.

That gap is where my interest now lies. Once the reader has said "that's interesting," what happens next?

## After the first surprise

It is easy to imagine a book spilling its contents onto a table: a building, an artifact, a landscape. Some of these interactions could be genuinely useful. Turning an object around might reveal a structure that a flat image conceals. Others may simply translate a sentence into a more expensive animation.

I do not think every playful moment needs to justify itself with a learning score. Delight, curiosity, and the desire to keep exploring are worthwhile. But if I claim to be designing a learning environment, I need a more specific account of what the interaction makes possible.

The distinction between mixed and virtual reality matters here. In mixed reality, the reader can still see the physical page and nearby objects. In an immersive virtual environment, the system must provide a visible counterpart for what the hands are holding. These are different reading situations, not interchangeable ways to make the same demo look impressive.

Nor is bringing books into virtual space a new idea. Billinghurst, Kato, and Poupyrev's [MagicBook](https://faculty.washington.edu/farkas/TC510-Fall2011/BillinghurstMagicBook.pdf) connected a physical book, augmented scenes, and immersive environments in 2001. It used page markers and authored virtual content, not an AI understanding arbitrary books. Later work on the [Magic Bookmark](https://www.surrey.ac.uk/research-projects/next-generation-paper-markets) explored page recognition through an augmented bookmark.

These precedents help locate the question. A new device does not make an old gesture a new contribution. But an old gesture can still support a reading practice worth investigating.

## A bookmark that carries a question

A bookmark usually remembers where I stopped. I am interested in what it would mean for it to remember *why* I stopped.

Consider an imagined reading session. One book describes a river crossing as decisive in a conflict. A second account places the crossing somewhere else. I mark the first passage, write down my uncertainty, and carry the bookmark to the second book.

In the design I have in mind, the bookmark would bring back the first passage alongside the new one. The reader could compare the accounts, inspect their sources, or leave the disagreement unresolved. A spatial view might hold the two maps side by side while keeping each attached to the passage that supports it.

The important action is not opening a portal. It is keeping a question alive across a change of context.

An AI could help retrieve a relevant passage or suggest a possible connection. It should not silently turn "these may concern the same event" into "these are equivalent accounts." The reader needs to be able to reject the match, inspect the original wording, and distinguish their own note from the system's suggestion.

Sometimes the most useful response would be no answer at all. The bookmark could preserve my first interpretation and let me return after reading more. The comparison would then be between two sources and between two moments in my own understanding.

This is a design sketch, not a feature I am claiming to have validated. Its value would depend on what readers actually do with it, including whether it distracts them from the text.

## The hundred-book test

The constraint that changes my thinking most is simple: what happens when there are a hundred books?

A bespoke scene for every chapter may be a wonderful work of interactive art. It is a difficult foundation for a reading platform. Each new book brings another production effort, and the books that can afford that effort get the richest treatment.

I would rather start with a small set of reusable actions: mark a claim, carry a question, compare accounts, examine an object, return to a source. The gesture stays familiar while the material changes. A reader should not have to learn a new interface every time they open a different history.

This does not make the content work disappear. Editions have different pagination. Translations make different choices. Two similar names may refer to different people. A source may be unavailable, or a passage may resist a neat visual reconstruction. Rights, source preparation, and editorial review remain real costs. Reusable interaction is not permission to automate away those judgments.

But it changes where the effort goes: from repeatedly manufacturing spectacle toward making many texts usable without pretending they say the same thing.

> The hundredth book should not require the hundredth game.

There is also a productive limit to reuse. A general interaction should be able to say that it has nothing useful to show. Not every paragraph needs a map. Sometimes keeping the reader with the sentence is the better design.

## When an object starts speaking

Another idea I keep returning to is a room in which ordinary objects become conversational partners. A book, a cup, or a stone could have a voice, a personality, and a story. The appeal is not only getting information. It is encountering something familiar as if it could answer back.

That could be a generous space for imagination. It also makes the boundary between interpretation and evidence unusually important.

Imagine a cup describing the journey by which it arrived on a table. Is it drawing on documented information about this particular object, explaining how similar objects are made, or improvising a fictional life? All three could be interesting. They are not the same kind of account.

A convincing voice can make a story feel like testimony. I would want the reader to move easily between the character and the material behind it: what is sourced, what is inferred, and what was invented for the encounter. Fiction need not be forbidden; it needs a recognizable boundary. A charming object should not acquire historical authority simply because it can speak in the first person.

The same boundary protects play. If an encounter is openly fictional, it can be strange, funny, and emotionally engaging without dressing itself as a lesson. And a room full of potential voices should still allow silence. The reader should choose which objects get their attention.

## Does this need a headset?

The strongest objection to the bookmark idea is that a tablet could do much of it. So could two books and a notebook.

That is not an objection to evade. It is the comparison the design needs. If a headset only adds friction to a good reading practice, the practice is worth keeping and the headset is not.

The possible advantage is more specific: a physical action could keep a question connected to an object, while a shared spatial arrangement could make several accounts easier to revisit. Whether that advantage outweighs setup, fatigue, expense, and distraction is an open question. A flat-screen alternative is not necessarily a lesser version.

I would begin with a small comparison using the same texts and source material in a paper-and-tablet workflow and a spatial one. Can readers explain why two accounts disagree? Can they find the passage behind a claim? Do they recognize an unsupported connection? Later, can they use the same comparison strategy on an unfamiliar text?

I would also ask what they enjoyed and what interrupted them. Enjoyment is a legitimate outcome; it should not be relabeled as comprehension. These are proposed evaluation questions, not results from a study I have completed.

## A small interface, a larger choice

For me, the societal computing question begins with the decision about what gets represented. A reading system that makes some accounts vivid and leaves others as plain text is already organizing attention. If it merges disagreements into a single fluent reconstruction, it is also making an editorial choice about authority.

Who chooses the sources? Can a reader introduce an account the system omitted? Can someone without the headset participate in the comparison? Can a teacher or community revise the interpretation without rebuilding the whole experience? These questions belong alongside tracking accuracy and visual fidelity, not after them.

This is where social science, AI, and engineering meet in a concrete problem. Social inquiry asks how people interpret and contest accounts. AI offers ways to retrieve and relate material, with its own uncertainties. Engineering turns those possibilities into actions people can accept, revise, or refuse. None of the three substitutes for the others.

I still want the moment of surprise when a paper page moves something in a virtual world. But I would like the experience to leave the reader with more than a memory of the effect: a question they can pursue, an account they can challenge, or a connection they can explain.

The book does not have to become a world. It might be enough for it to become a better place to begin a question.
