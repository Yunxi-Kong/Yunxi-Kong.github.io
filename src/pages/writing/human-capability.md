Imagine an assistant that finishes your work before you wake up. It has read the papers, drafted the proposal, arranged the meetings, and chosen what you should do next. Everything is excellent. There is only one awkward question: whose project is this becoming?

I do not ask that question because I think meaningful work must be difficult, or because I want people to compete with machines at tasks machines do better. I ask because a system can increase our output while narrowing our authorship of our own lives. Those are different changes, and we need a language for telling them apart.

## More capable, not merely more productive

The phrase I keep returning to is a *cognitive exoskeleton*: an external system that helps a person remember, understand, explore, coordinate, and act. Not a mechanical suit, and not a claim that every kind of thinking should be automated. It is a way to ask what a durable relationship with intelligent tools could make possible.

The ambition is older than today's models. Douglas Engelbart's [augmentation framework](https://www.dougengelbart.org/pubs/augment-3906.html) treated people, tools, language, methods, and training as parts of a larger system. What matters is not the machine in isolation, but what a person can accomplish within that arrangement.

My own emphasis is on the relationship between capability and agency. By capability, I mean the practical ability to understand something or bring about a result. By agency, I mean having a meaningful role in choosing the goal, questioning the means, and changing course. A tool can improve one without improving the other.

Consider two hypothetical assistants helping a student choose a research direction. One ranks topics and delivers a finished plan. The other also produces a plan, but makes the tradeoffs available: which questions fit the student's interests, which depend on unavailable data, and which would require learning a new method. Both may save time. Only the second is designed to help the student participate in the choice.

That distinction is not a demand to inspect every intermediate step. A person can exercise agency through sensible delegation. The question is whether delegation leaves them a usable way to disagree.

## Memory should remain editable

A useful personal system would need continuity. It should not require us to explain our priorities from the beginning every morning. It might connect a question asked on a walk with a paper read at a desk, or recognize that an abandoned idea has become feasible under new circumstances.

But continuity creates an unusually intimate design problem. A remembered preference can become a prediction; a prediction can become a default; a default can quietly become a constraint. An assistant that knows what I wanted last year may be very efficient at steering me toward a life I no longer want.

The answer cannot simply be more memory. The person needs to see what the system believes about them, correct it, and decide what should not travel into another context. An idea explored in private is not a commitment. A goal postponed is not necessarily a goal abandoned. A preference inferred by a model is not a preference the person has endorsed.

> A system that remembers me should also leave room for me to change.

This changes how I think about the devices involved. A phone, computer, watch, or spatial interface might each provide a different point of access. None should require a total account of a person's life merely to complete a bounded task. The interesting infrastructure is not an all-seeing assistant. It is context that can be used selectively, under authority that can be withdrawn.

## Understanding needs something to push against

There is another limit to assistance by explanation: an answer can feel clear before a person has discovered where their understanding fails.

Suppose someone is trying to understand how a landscape shapes movement between two settlements. A paragraph can explain the importance of a river crossing. An interactive environment could let them move the crossing and examine which routes become possible, which become costly, and who is left farther away. The point is not that a three-dimensional scene is inherently better than prose. The point is that changing a condition can expose a relationship that fluent description leaves passive.

This is part of why I build interactive environments. My work on historical annotation and narrative worlds gives me concrete places to ask how people encounter alternatives and express an interpretation. It does not yet establish that these environments produce deeper understanding. That is a research question, not something a compelling demonstration can settle.

An especially interesting possibility is reciprocal learning. A person learns the structure of a problem while the system learns which explanation, representation, or intervention is useful to that person. The two processes could improve each other. They could also go wrong together: a system might learn to make someone feel understood while avoiding the disagreement that would actually help them learn.

So responsiveness alone is not enough. I would want to know whether a person can recognize a changed assumption, distinguish a plausible explanation from a sound one, and take their understanding into a setting the system has not rehearsed.

## The right to delegate, and the right to stop

A cognitive exoskeleton cannot remain only a source of suggestions. Eventually it must help people do things: operate tools, coordinate work, and carry a decision into the world. Yet the moment a system can act, the question changes from whether an answer is useful to whether an action is authorized.

There is a large difference between preparing a message, recommending that it be sent, and sending it. A good system should not erase that difference just because it can complete all three steps. Nor should it demand a confirmation for every trivial action. Constant approval requests can turn supposed oversight into a habit of clicking through.

The design problem is selective delegation: which decisions fit within an agreed scope, which require renewed permission, and what should happen when circumstances change. Reversibility matters. So does the ability to pause a process without having to understand the whole system first.

Sometimes the most capable assistant should do nothing. It should let a person finish thinking, leave an unformed idea unoptimized, or accept that a conversation is not a task to be completed. Silence is not a failure of intelligence when intervention would undermine the activity being supported.

## Dependence is not the only question

The obvious objection to this vision is that external support makes people dependent. But dependence by itself is too blunt a test. Reading depends on written language; scientific reasoning depends on instruments and other people's work. We should not judge every tool by whether its user could reproduce the result unaided.

The sharper distinction is between useful reliance and a relationship the person cannot question or leave. Someone may appropriately rely on a tool while retaining the ability to detect when it is unreliable, ask for another account, and move their work elsewhere. Someone else may retain many unaided skills while having little practical control over the systems that organize their options.

That is why I would evaluate more than immediate performance. I would also look at whether people can redirect assistance, recover from errors, understand consequential choices, and preserve access to their own context. Where learning is the goal, retention and transfer matter. Where access or execution is the goal, useful accomplishment with support may be the right outcome.

The social question follows directly. Who can afford this infrastructure? Who controls its memory? Can a person carry their context to a different provider, or does a more helpful assistant also make leaving more expensive? Individual augmentation could broaden opportunity while deepening dependence on a small number of institutions.

For me, that is where human-AI interaction becomes societal computing. The object of study is not only a person using an interface. It is the distribution of understanding, authority, and opportunity that the interface helps create.

I want to build systems that give people more room to act. The difficult part is making sure that the room remains theirs.
