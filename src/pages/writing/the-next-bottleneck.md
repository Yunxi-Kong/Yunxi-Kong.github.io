Imagine that producing a good plan becomes almost free. A neighborhood group can generate a hundred proposals for a new public space: drawings, budgets, maintenance schedules, and arguments for each alternative. What does it still lack?

Perhaps permission to use the land. Perhaps agreement about whose needs matter. Perhaps someone willing to maintain the space after the opening day. The group has gained a remarkable capacity to produce proposals. It has not automatically gained the capacity to make one real.

That gap is where I want to work.

## When one constraint loosens

My way into AI begins with a question from economics: when something valuable becomes easier to produce, what becomes scarce around it?

This is not a prediction that intelligence will soon be free, or that today's models can solve every difficult problem. It is a thought experiment for choosing research problems. If a capability improved dramatically and its cost fell, which of the surrounding difficulties would shrink with it? Which would remain? Which would become more important because people could now attempt more?

Consider a researcher who can suddenly commission ten promising analyses instead of one. The gain may be substantial. But the researcher must still decide whether the analyses answer the right question, whether their assumptions are compatible, and which result is worth investigating. More supply can expose a new constraint instead of eliminating constraints altogether.

There is already suggestive evidence of this tension. In its October 2026 [study of employment inside Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/employment-at-anthropic), the company describes human attention becoming a constraint as agent output expands. It also discusses selection into tool adoption and the limits of interpreting output measures. This is evidence from one unusual organization, not a universal forecast. It does, however, make the question concrete: what changes when generating work becomes easier than deciding what to do with it?

The answer will differ across settings. A small organization may still lack reliable technical help. A large one may have plenty of analysis but struggle with coordination. There is no necessary historical ladder from information to attention to institutions. These constraints coexist. The useful task is to identify which one is binding for whom.

## An answer is not an opportunity

A student can receive excellent advice about research and still lack access to a laboratory. A worker can obtain a well-reasoned recommendation and still lack authority to change a process. A community can understand a problem and still be excluded from the decision that governs it.

These are not simply examples of imperfect AI. Even an accurate answer cannot, by itself, confer a right, create a budget, or settle a conflict over whose interests should prevail.

This is why I care about the distinction between the capability of a model and the capability of a person in a setting. The first concerns what a system can produce. The second depends on what the person can understand, use, contest, and act upon, given the resources and relationships around them.

An interface can help connect the two. It might make assumptions inspectable, help someone prepare an alternative, or enable action that previously required specialist support. But it can also narrow the range of visible choices. A highly polished recommendation may make a disputed decision look like a technical conclusion.

> Cheaper intelligence does not settle who gets to decide what it is for.

This is not a reason to give up on better tools. It is a reason to design them with a more demanding outcome in mind. I want to ask whether people gain useful options, not merely whether they receive more output.

## One person, many agents

Now extend the thought experiment. Suppose one person can direct a hundred agents. Some investigate, some build, some test, and some negotiate with other services. Does that person now have the capability of a hundred-person organization?

Only if the work fits together.

If every agent independently produces a report, the person may inherit a hundred reading assignments. If agents share information without preserving differences in assumptions, they may converge on the same mistake. If each optimizes its local task, the overall project may become less coherent even while every component looks successful.

The interesting variable is not agent count. It is the organization of the work: who knows what, who may commit a shared resource, how disagreements are resolved, and which errors reach the person in time to matter.

That brings economic and social questions inside the technical system. Information allocation becomes a design choice. So do authority, accountability, and the cost of changing direction. A multi-agent architecture is, in part, an arrangement for deciding whose information and actions can affect everyone else.

One possibility is that these systems let much smaller teams undertake ambitious work. Another is that coordinating many agents rewards organizations that already possess infrastructure, data, and distribution. Both effects could occur at once. Greater individual productivity does not necessarily mean a more equal distribution of power.

## Why not automate the bottleneck too?

There is a strong objection to this argument: AI can also summarize reports, check analyses, organize teams, and help negotiate agreements. Why assume that attention or coordination must remain human bottlenecks?

I do not. Those tasks are legitimate targets for automation. If a system can reliably resolve routine disagreements or direct attention to the few consequential decisions, that is a real improvement. A useful research agenda should not protect a human role merely because it exists today.

But automating a step can change the location and character of the problem. If an agent selects which evidence I see, I need ways to understand and challenge that selection. If a platform coordinates everyone's agents, its rules become part of the environment in which everyone acts. A technical solution can create a new dependency even as it removes an old difficulty.

Some constraints also involve incompatible interests rather than missing computation. Better analysis can reveal the consequences of two policies without making the people affected agree on which consequence is acceptable. Treating that disagreement as an error to be optimized away would be a substantive political choice, not a neutral engineering improvement.

The point is not that every solved problem creates an equally hard replacement. Sometimes an improvement simply makes life better. The point is to examine the whole chain before equating a cheaper component with a better social outcome.

## A research agenda across three scales

For me, these questions connect at three scales. At the level of the person, I want to understand when intelligent assistance expands understanding and the ability to act. At the level of the group, I want to study how people and agents coordinate knowledge and commitments. At the institutional level, I want to examine who gains access, who becomes dependent, and how the benefits are distributed.

These are not three unrelated application areas. A design that changes how one person delegates can also change how a team distributes responsibility. A platform rule can determine which forms of delegation are possible in the first place.

My approach brings together **social science, AI, and engineering**. Social science helps specify a mechanism rather than merely announce that technology will change society. AI provides new possibilities for assistance, action, and simulation. Engineering makes an arrangement concrete enough to inspect, use, and challenge. Empirical work then asks which of the intended changes actually occurred.

For example, I would like to compare different ways of organizing the same agent capabilities: not just which produces the most output, but which makes important disagreements visible, allows a person to redirect work, and keeps coordination costs manageable. That is a proposed line of inquiry, not a result I have already established.

This is what draws me to societal computing. I do not want to study technical systems as if the institutions around them were fixed, or study institutions as if the tools available to people were fixed. Both can change together.

The question I want to keep asking is simple enough to carry from one project to another: when a machine becomes more capable, what has become possible for the people around it, and who gets to choose?
