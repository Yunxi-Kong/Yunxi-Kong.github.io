Suppose an AI can compose a piece of music better than I can. It can explain the harmony, plan my practice, and generate an excellent performance. Which part of learning to play the piano has it completed for me?

The answer depends on what I wanted. If I needed a recording, perhaps the task is finished. If I wanted to hear something beautiful, listening may be enough. If I wanted to feel a phrase take shape under my hands, something important has not happened yet.

This distinction is one reason I am reluctant to build my research interests around a list of tasks that machines supposedly will never perform. I would rather ask what becomes valuable for people even if machine performance becomes extraordinary.

## Not a claim about the limits of machine consciousness

There is a tempting argument: humans have experience, machines do not, therefore some territory will always belong exclusively to us. I do not need that argument for the question I care about.

Even if a future AI had experiences of its own, its experience would not thereby become mine. A friend can describe a journey vividly, but hearing the description and making the journey remain different events in my life. The same distinction applies to an account generated by a machine.

This is a modest point about the purpose of an activity, not a proof that a certain kind of intelligence is impossible. It leaves room for AI to create environments, explanations, and forms of assistance that make new experiences possible. It does not require those experiences to be unmediated or technologically pure.

The question shifts from "What can the machine never do?" to "What do we want to happen for the person?"

## Three outcomes that can look alike from a distance

Imagine a system that produces a beautiful drawing, a system that helps someone learn to draw, and a system that lets someone spend an absorbing hour exploring visual ideas. They may use similar tools. They are pursuing different outcomes.

  <div><dt>Capability</dt><dd>A person can do or understand something they could not before.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>Experience</dt><dd>Something meaningful has happened in the person's life.</dd></div>

An excellent artifact does not establish that its user gained a capability. An improved capability does not guarantee that the activity was meaningful. A meaningful encounter need not produce a marketable artifact at all.

These outcomes can reinforce one another. Completing a drawing may encourage further practice; learning a technique may make new forms of expression available. But if we measure only the finished drawing, we can miss the difference between supporting the person and quietly replacing the activity they came to do.

The distinction also prevents a different mistake: insisting that everyone should value the same outcome. Someone who wants a poster does not owe the tool a commitment to become an illustrator. Someone who wants to draw should not be told that a generated poster makes their effort pointless.

## Education after the easiest justification changes

One way to justify education is that it prepares people to perform valuable work. That remains important. But I do not think it exhausts what education is for.

Consider a future in which many tasks can be performed more cheaply by machines. This is a thought experiment, not a prediction about a date or an occupation. Would it follow that people should stop learning the things involved?

Only if the sole purpose of learning were to supply those outputs. A person might still want to understand a scientific explanation, participate in a public decision, recognize a misleading claim, appreciate a form of art, or discover an activity they did not know they could enjoy.

Learning changes which parts of the world are available to us. Someone who learns to hear a musical structure may encounter a familiar piece differently. Someone who learns a language may enter conversations that translation alone did not make equally available. That does not make assistance inferior. It means assistance and cultivation can serve different purposes.

This is why I think of education as one setting for a broader concern with human capability. The same question appears in creative tools, spatial environments, scientific systems, and public services: does the technology only deliver a result, or does it also expand how a person can encounter and act in the world?

## Effort is not automatically meaningful

There is an obvious danger in this argument. It can romanticize difficulty and turn an unnecessary burden into a moral achievement.

I do not think an inaccessible instrument is more authentic than an adapted one. I do not think struggling with an interface is a necessary part of understanding the subject behind it. Assistance can remove obstacles that have nothing to do with the experience a person values.

At the piano, a tool might make notation easier to follow, adapt a practice sequence, or enable a form of performance that a conventional instrument excludes. The person is still having an experience. Technology does not make it counterfeit.

The useful distinction is between friction that prevents participation and effort that is part of the chosen activity. Even that boundary is personal and changeable. On one day I may want to practice; on another I may want to listen. A system should not impose a single theory of self-improvement on both occasions.

## When good content is plentiful

If high-quality material became much easier to produce, people would not acquire unlimited time to experience it. The question of selection would become more important: what is worth attending to, and who helps define worth?

A recommendation system can help someone discover a new interest. It can also become very good at supplying more of what already captures their attention. Satisfaction with the next item is not the same as a life whose range of experiences is expanding.

I am interested in tools that leave room for the person's taste to develop. That might mean revisiting an initial judgment, encountering something unfamiliar, or choosing to stay with a difficult work instead of immediately replacing it with an easier one. It might also mean choosing pleasure without turning every hour into a developmental project.

There is no simple score that settles all of this. That does not make careful inquiry impossible. It means we should be explicit about which aspect we are measuring and which judgment remains with the person.

> More available experiences are not the same as a richer life. The connection has to be made by someone living it.

## Designing for an encounter, not an effect

This also changes how I think about VR. An immersive environment can aim to show something, help someone learn something, or create an encounter with a scale or perspective they rarely experience. Those are distinct ambitions.

A dramatic reaction in a headset would not establish a durable change in understanding or values. Equally, the absence of a measurable productivity gain would not make an experience worthless. A system needs an honest account of its purpose before choosing what counts as success.

For an exploratory study, I would want participants' own accounts of what mattered, alongside measures appropriate to the specific claim. I would ask about unwanted effects and whether the experience remained meaningful after the novelty faded. I would not treat time spent, excitement, or a single questionnaire as a complete account of what happened. This is a research orientation, not a claim that I have established an effect.

The institutional question follows: who can access these experiences, who designs their range, and how much freedom does a person have to refuse the suggested path? A world of abundant generated content could widen participation while making the environments through which people encounter it more tightly controlled.

I want to work on technologies that expand what people can understand, make, and experience. Their purpose should not depend on finding a permanent weakness in AI. Even if machines become much more capable than we can currently imagine, there will still be a question about what we are making possible for the people who live alongside them.
